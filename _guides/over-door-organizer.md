<p class="lede">An organizer changes a door from a moving barrier into a loaded storage surface. Evaluate the door as a system before counting pockets or hooks.</p>

<h2>Test the gap with the door closed</h2>

<p>Measure the clearance above the door and between the door and frame. Brackets need enough space to pass without scraping, but overly loose brackets can rattle. Weather stripping, closers, and uneven frames can reduce clearance in one section even when another section looks generous.</p>

<p>Mock up the bracket thickness with a few layers of removable card and close the door slowly. Stop if resistance changes. A bedroom or bathroom door that no longer latches is not an acceptable storage tradeoff.</p>

<h2>Account for working depth</h2>

<p>The organizer’s listed depth may exclude the items placed in it. Shoes, bottles, and rolled towels can project farther than a pocket. On the opening side, that projection may strike a wall, handle, shelf, or person standing behind the door. On the closing side, contents can press against hanging clothes or fixtures.</p>

<p>Mark the loaded depth with tape or a cardboard box. Move the door through its full arc and test access from both sides. Also check whether the added thickness narrows a required path.</p>

<h2>Consider the door and hinge load</h2>

<p>Follow the organizer manufacturer’s load limit and any instructions from the door manufacturer or property manager. Hollow-core doors and older hinges are not equivalent to solid doors. Even a modest total load can become stressful when concentrated at the top and repeatedly accelerated during a swing.</p>

<p>Distribute light items and avoid fragile liquids, heavy tools, glass, or anything that could injure someone if it falls. If the door begins to sag, scrape, or move differently, unload the organizer rather than adjusting building hardware without permission.</p>

<h2>Choose the pocket geometry</h2>

<p>Clear pockets improve visibility but can trap dust and may stretch under bulky items. Fabric pockets accommodate irregular shapes but hide small objects. Open hooks work for frequently used lightweight items but can create noise. Choose a format based on one category, not the promise that it can store anything.</p>

<p>High pockets may be unreachable and low pockets can interfere with pets or children. Do not store medication, chemicals, sharp objects, or choking hazards where unauthorized users can reach them.</p>

<aside class="decision-box">
<h3>Before buying</h3>

<ul><li>Confirm top and side clearances through the full swing.</li><li>Mock up loaded depth, not just empty organizer depth.</li><li>Set a conservative weight budget.</li><li>Check that the door latches and does not scrape.</li><li>Use the organizer for one lightweight category.</li></ul>
</aside>

<h2>Know when to choose a different tool</h2>

<p>If the door is a fire-rated assembly, has a closer, already rubs the frame, or must seal for sound or climate control, avoid adding hardware unless explicitly approved. A slim rolling cart, under-shelf basket, or small freestanding rack may occupy visible space but create fewer mechanical problems.</p>
