<p class="lede">A deep drawer can hide small kitchen tools beneath a pile of larger ones. The useful fix depends on the problem: a sliding upper bin separates levels, a peg tray gives lightweight containers boundaries, and a small open box keeps loose parts together. These three removable options do not require drilling into a rental kitchen.</p>

<p>These are alternatives for different jobs, not a kit guaranteed to fit together. Empty the drawer, remove duplicates, and separate everyday items from occasional-use tools. If an existing container solves the problem, keep it. An organizer should make the next item easier to retrieve, not merely divide the same crowded pile.</p>

<h2 id="measure-the-moving-space">Measure the moving space, not just the empty box</h2>

<p>Measure the narrowest usable width and depth inside the drawer. Then check the opening it travels through: a tall front panel does not mean there is that much clearance beneath the cabinet rail. Include the height of the stored items, not only the organizer. A cardboard footprint can help you check a proposed layout before ordering.</p>

<p>For a frame-mounted bin, also inspect the opposing top edges that will support its tabs. A drawer with an interrupted rim, a rail in the way, or incompatible side construction may not work even when its width looks suitable. Do not force a drawer shut to test clearance. None of these selections establishes a universal fit or a tested load capacity for your drawer.</p>

<h2 id="use-an-upper-layer">1. Use an upper layer for frequently reached tools</h2>

<p>DrawerFit is the option to consider when there is useful space above the contents and the drawer has suitable supporting edges. It can separate a small daily-use group from the larger items below. It is less compelling if you must move everything in the drawer before the bin can slide.</p>

<h2 id="give-lightweight-containers-boundaries">2. Give lightweight containers adjustable boundaries</h2>

<p>Peggy suits a collection whose shapes do not fit fixed compartments. Arrange the pegs around the containers you actually keep, rather than trying to fill every available position. The open layout lets you change those boundaries when the collection changes.</p>

<p><strong>The tradeoff:</strong> Pegs mark boundaries but do not enclose tiny loose parts. They also use floor area, so a tightly packed drawer may hold fewer items after installation. Measure containers in their intended orientation and keep their tops below the closing clearance. Do not infer that the four-inch organizer height is enough for whatever sits between the pegs.</p>

<p>Begin with one category, such as lightweight food containers and their lids. Leave a finger gap around the pieces you remove most often. You do not have to put both trays in the same drawer; draw the full two-tray footprint before deciding on that layout.</p>

<h2 id="contain-one-small-category">3. Contain one small category in a fixed box</h2>

<p>A small bin is the simplest choice when the problem is loose parts rather than unused upper space. Keep clips, measuring accessories, or another modest group together. Unlike a full-width tray, this leaves the rest of the drawer available for items that need a different layout.</p>
